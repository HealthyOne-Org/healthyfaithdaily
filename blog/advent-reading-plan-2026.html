Advent Reading Plan 2026: Four Weeks From the Prophets to the Manger

November 10, 2026 · 8 min read · By Chris Hardaway
Photo via Pexels

Advent is the season of waiting. The word comes from the Latin adventus, "coming" or "arrival," and for centuries Western churches have spent the four Sundays before Christmas reading the promises God made long before Bethlehem. In 2026, Advent begins on Sunday, November 29, the Sunday after Thanksgiving, and Christmas Day falls on a Friday.

Below is a four-week Advent reading plan for 2026 with one passage a day, from the prophets to the manger. Each reading takes five to ten minutes. Notes after the plan explain how to read it as a family, with small children or with teenagers, and what to do if you fall behind.

What Advent is (and what it is not)

Advent is not commanded in Scripture. It is a church tradition, observed in Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, Lutheran, Methodist and many other churches, and increasingly in evangelical ones. In the Western calendar it begins on the fourth Sunday before Christmas. The Eastern Orthodox churches keep a longer season, the Nativity Fast, which starts on November 15.

Advent has always looked two directions at once. It remembers the first coming of Christ, when Israel waited centuries for the Messiah, and it looks ahead to His second coming, which the church still waits for. The New Testament often uses the Greek word parousia (Strong's G3952), "coming" or "presence," for that second arrival, as in James 5:8: "Be ye also patient; stablish your hearts: for the coming of the Lord draweth nigh." Reading the prophets in December puts you in Israel's place: you know the promise, and you are waiting to see it kept.

How this plan is built

Many churches give each Sunday a theme and light a candle on an Advent wreath. The most common set today is hope, peace, joy and love, though traditions differ; some name the candles for the prophets, Bethlehem, the shepherds and the angels instead. This plan follows the hope, peace, joy, love order loosely:

  • Week 1, Hope (Nov 29–Dec 5): the first promises, from Eden to David's throne.
  • Week 2, Peace (Dec 6–12): the prophets who said a messenger would prepare the way.
  • Week 3, Joy (Dec 13–19): the announcements to Zacharias, Mary and Joseph, and the songs they sang.
  • Week 4, Love (Dec 20–24): why the Son came, ending with the birth itself on Christmas Eve.

Week 1: Hope — the promise (Nov 29–Dec 5)

  1. Sun, Nov 29 – Genesis 3:8–15. The first promise comes right after the first sin: the seed of the woman will bruise the serpent's head. Christians have long read this as the first hint of the gospel.
  2. Mon, Nov 30 – Genesis 12:1–3. God promises Abram that "in thee shall all families of the earth be blessed."
  3. Tue, Dec 1 – 2 Samuel 7:8–16. God promises David a house and a throne "established for ever."
  4. Wed, Dec 2 – Isaiah 9:1–7. "The people that walked in darkness have seen a great light." Read it slowly; it is the center of the week.
  5. Thu, Dec 3 – Isaiah 11:1–10. A rod out of the stem of Jesse, and a world where the wolf dwells with the lamb.
  6. Fri, Dec 4 – Micah 5:2–5. The ruler of Israel will come from little Bethlehem.
  7. Sat, Dec 5 – Isaiah 40:1–11. "Comfort ye, comfort ye my people." The shepherd will gather the lambs with His arm.

For unto us a child is born, unto us a son is given: and the government shall be upon his shoulder: and his name shall be called Wonderful, Counsellor, The mighty God, The everlasting Father, The Prince of Peace.

Isaiah 9:6 (KJV)

The title "Messiah" is the Hebrew mashiach (H4899), "anointed one." Kings and priests in Israel were anointed with oil. The Greek translation of that word is Christos (G5547), which is why "Christ" is a title before it is a name. When you read 2 Samuel 7 and Isaiah 9 this week, you are reading where that hope for an anointed King comes from.

Week 2: Peace — preparing the way (Dec 6–12)

  1. Sun, Dec 6 – Malachi 3:1–4 and 4:5–6. The last words of the Old Testament promise a messenger before the Lord comes.
  2. Mon, Dec 7 – Isaiah 7:10–14. The sign of a child called Immanuel.
  3. Tue, Dec 8 – Jeremiah 23:5–6. A righteous Branch called "THE LORD OUR RIGHTEOUSNESS."
  4. Wed, Dec 9 – Luke 1:5–25. After four hundred years of prophetic silence, an angel speaks to an old priest named Zacharias.
  5. Thu, Dec 10 – Mark 1:1–8. John the Baptist, the voice in the wilderness Isaiah and Malachi described.
  6. Fri, Dec 11 – Isaiah 35:1–10. The desert blossoms; the eyes of the blind are opened.
  7. Sat, Dec 12 – Psalm 85. "Mercy and truth are met together; righteousness and peace have kissed each other" (85:10). Notice the selah after verse 2 and pause there.

A note on Isaiah 7:14. The Hebrew word the KJV renders "virgin" is almah (H5959), which means a young woman of marriageable age. Scholars disagree about whether the word itself specifies virginity. The Greek translation of the Old Testament used before Jesus' time rendered it parthenos (G3933), "virgin," and Matthew 1:23 quotes that wording. Christians across traditions confess the virgin birth on the basis of Matthew and Luke. The name itself, Immanuel (H6005), means "God with us," as Matthew explains.

Week 3: Joy — the announcements (Dec 13–19)

  1. Sun, Dec 13 – Luke 1:26–38. Gabriel comes to Mary. "Be it unto me according to thy word."
  2. Mon, Dec 14 – Luke 1:39–56. Mary visits Elisabeth and sings: "My soul doth magnify the Lord."
  3. Tue, Dec 15 – Matthew 1:1–17. The genealogy. Do not skip it. Look for Tamar, Rahab, Ruth and "her that had been the wife of Urias."
  4. Wed, Dec 16 – Matthew 1:18–25. Joseph's dream and his quiet obedience.
  5. Thu, Dec 17 – Luke 1:57–80. John is born, and Zacharias, silent for nine months, sings of "the dayspring from on high."
  6. Fri, Dec 18 – Zephaniah 3:14–20. God "will rejoice over thee with joy; he will rest in his love, he will joy over thee with singing" (3:17).
  7. Sat, Dec 19 – Isaiah 61:1–3, 10–11. Good tidings to the meek, the passage Jesus read aloud in Nazareth (Luke 4:16–21).

The third Sunday of Advent has long been a day of joy in the church calendar. In Catholic and Anglican churches it is called Gaudete Sunday, from the Latin for "rejoice," and the third candle on many wreaths is pink or rose instead of purple.

Week 4: Love — the coming (Dec 20–25)

  1. Sun, Dec 20 – John 1:1–14. "And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us."
  2. Mon, Dec 21 – Philippians 2:5–11. He "made himself of no reputation, and took upon him the form of a servant."
  3. Tue, Dec 22 – Galatians 4:4–7. "When the fulness of the time was come, God sent forth his Son."
  4. Wed, Dec 23 – Hebrews 1:1–4. God, who spoke through the prophets, "hath in these last days spoken unto us by his Son."
  5. Thu, Dec 24 (Christmas Eve) – Luke 2:1–20. The birth, the shepherds, the angels. Read it aloud, all of it.
  6. Fri, Dec 25 (Christmas Day) – 1 John 4:9–10 and Titus 2:11–14. "In this was manifested the love of God toward us." Titus ends Advent where it began, "looking for that blessed hope," the second coming.

Advent is not about reading more. It is about reading in order. When you walk from Genesis 3 to Luke 2 over four weeks, the manger stops being a scene and becomes an answer to promises God made over thousands of years.

If you want to keep going after Christmas, Matthew 2:1–12 (the wise men) is traditionally read at Epiphany, January 6.

Reading the plan as a family

  • Keep it short and the same time every day. After dinner works for most families. Light the candle, read, pray one sentence, blow the candle out. Ten minutes is enough.
  • Small children: read only the key verse or two and ask one question: "What did God promise?" On days with long passages, tell the story in your own words and then read the verse.
  • Older children and teens: let them read aloud and take turns choosing one word that stood out. Ask where the promise in the Old Testament reading shows up again in the Gospels.
  • Keep a list. Write each day's promise on a card or in a journal. By Christmas Eve you will have twenty-six of them to read back before Luke 2.
  • If you fall behind, skip ahead. Read today's passage, not the backlog. Advent is a season of patience, including with yourself.

If your family started a gratitude list over Thanksgiving, Advent is a natural next step. Our Bible verses for Thanksgiving end with Psalm 103:2, "forget not all his benefits," and Advent is four weeks of remembering what God promised.

FAQ

When does Advent start in 2026?

In Western churches Advent begins on the fourth Sunday before Christmas. Christmas 2026 falls on a Friday, so Advent starts on Sunday, November 29, 2026, and the four Sundays are November 29 and December 6, 13 and 20. Eastern Orthodox churches keep a longer Nativity Fast that begins on November 15.

What are the four themes of Advent?

The most common set today is hope, peace, joy and love, one for each Sunday and each candle on the Advent wreath. Traditions differ: some name the candles for the prophets, Bethlehem, the shepherds and the angels. The third Sunday is often a day of joy, called Gaudete Sunday in Catholic and Anglican churches.

What Bible verses are read during Advent?

Advent readings usually come from the prophets who promised the Messiah, such as Isaiah 9:1-7, Isaiah 11:1-10, Micah 5:2 and Malachi 3:1, then from the announcements in Luke 1 and Matthew 1, ending with the birth of Jesus in Luke 2:1-20 on Christmas Eve.

Is Advent in the Bible?

No. Advent is a church tradition, not a season commanded in Scripture. The word comes from the Latin adventus, meaning coming or arrival. Many Christians keep it because it gives four weeks to read the promises of Christ's first coming and to look ahead to His second coming, which the New Testament calls the parousia (G3952).

The bottom line

Advent is four weeks of reading God's promises in order, from the first hint of a Savior in Genesis 3 to the shepherds in Luke 2. Start on Sunday, November 29, read one passage a day, write down each promise, and by Christmas Eve the manger will read like the answer it is.
