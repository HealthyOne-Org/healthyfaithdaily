How to Take Sermon Notes You'll Actually Reread (2026 Guide)

October 29, 2026 · 7 min read · By Chris Hardaway
Photo via Pexels

Most sermon notes die in a drawer. You write three bullet points and a verse reference during the message, feel good about it, and never open the page again. By Wednesday you can't remember the second point. That is not a discipline problem. It is a method problem. Notes you will reread have to be written for the person you'll be on Wednesday, not the person sitting in the pew on Sunday.

This is the method I use, and the one Healthy Faith's sermon notes feature is built around. It takes about five minutes before the service, light attention during it, and ten minutes after. The payoff is a page you can actually use all week.

Why most sermon notes don't work

Three things go wrong, usually all at once:

  • You transcribe instead of listening. Trying to write every point means you catch the words and miss the message. Nobody rereads a transcript.
  • You write for Sunday, not Wednesday. "Trust God" means something while the pastor is saying it. On its own on a page, it means nothing.
  • There is no step after the service. The notes end when the sermon ends, and so does the sermon.

The fix for all three is the same: write less during the message, and write more after it.

Before the sermon: five minutes

1. Read the passage first

If your church publishes the text ahead of time, or follows a series, read the chapter before you arrive. Not studying it, just reading it once so the words are familiar when you hear them. If you don't know the passage, read it as the pastor announces it, while the introduction is still going. Those thirty seconds change how much you understand of the next thirty minutes.

2. Set up the page

Draw a line across the top third of the page, or if you're on your phone, write three headings. The top section is for the sermon. The bottom is for you. Keep them separate, because the thing you take home from a sermon is rarely the main point; it's the one sentence that landed on your situation.

3. Write one question

At the top, write what you're carrying into the room this week. A decision, a worry, a relationship. "What do I do about the job." "Why am I so tired of church." Sermons answer questions you bring. If you bring none, you get a lecture.

During the sermon: write less

The rule: catch the passages, the points, and the one sentence. Nothing else.

Passages

Write every Scripture reference the pastor reads or cites, in order. Just the reference, not the text. This is the skeleton of the sermon and the thing you'll want most when you reread, because it's what you can open and study yourself. A good sermon is a guided tour of four or five passages; your notes should let you take the tour again.

Points

Most sermons have two to four main points, and most pastors signal them: a slide changes, the voice changes, "the second thing we see here is." Write each one as a complete sentence, not a fragment. "Faith grows in the waiting" is reusable on Wednesday. "Waiting" is not.

The one sentence

Somewhere in every sermon there's a line that hits you specifically. You'll feel it. Write it in the bottom section, word for word if you can, and put a star next to it. Then go back to listening. Don't try to explain it yet. That's the after-step.

That's the whole during-list. If you're writing more than this, you're transcribing. Put the pen down and listen until the next passage or point.

After the sermon: ten minutes, the same day

This is the step almost everyone skips, and it's the one that makes the notes worth keeping. Do it in the car, over lunch, or that evening. Not Monday. By Monday the sermon is already fading.

1. Write the message in one sentence

At the top of the page, above everything: what was the sermon about, in your words, in one sentence a friend would understand. "Joseph forgave his brothers because he could see what God was doing with the wrong they did." If you can't do this, you either didn't understand the sermon or it didn't have a center. Either is useful to know.

2. Answer your question

Go back to the question you wrote before the service. Did the sermon speak to it? Write two or three lines. Sometimes the answer is "not directly, but the second point applies." Sometimes it's "no, but Psalm 37 did." Both are real answers. If one word from the text keeps catching you, look it up; our word study on Selah shows what that looks like, and if the sermon met you in a worried season, keep these Bible verses for anxiety close.

3. Explain the starred sentence

Why did it land? What in your week does it touch? This is the most personal part of the page and the part you'll reread most. Be honest. Nobody else reads this.

4. Pick one thing to carry into the week

Not three. One. A verse to memorize, a conversation to have, a habit to start, a person to forgive. Write it as an action with a day attached: "Call Mom Tuesday." "Read Genesis 50 again Thursday morning." If it isn't specific, it won't happen, and the sermon will have been an hour of agreement with no consequence.

5. Set a reminder

Put the one thing, and the one-sentence message, into whatever will show it to you midweek. A calendar event, a lock-screen note, an alarm labeled with the verse. The point of notes is to be read later, so arrange for later to happen.

A one-page template

Copy this into a notebook or a note on your phone. Fill in the top section after, the middle during, the bottom after.

Date / Preacher / Series
In one sentence: ______ (fill in after)
My question this week: ______ (before)

Passages: ______
Point 1: ______
Point 2: ______
Point 3: ______
★ The sentence: ______

Why it landed: ______
Answer to my question: ______
One thing this week (with a day): ______

If you listen rather than write

Some people can't take notes and listen at the same time; writing pulls them out of the message. If that's you, don't fight it. Record the sermon instead (ask first if your church has a policy, though most publish their services anyway), and do the whole method afterward from the recording. You'll need to find the passages and points on replay, which takes longer, but you'll have been fully present during the service, and that matters more.

Rereading: the habit that makes it all worth it

Once a month, read back through the one-sentence summaries at the top of each page. Ten sermons in ten sentences. You will see things no single Sunday shows you: what your church has been teaching, what questions you keep bringing, which "one thing" you actually did. That's the difference between attending sermons and being formed by them. The notes were never the point. The person reading them on Wednesday was.

FAQ

Should I take notes during a sermon or just listen?

Take light notes: the Scripture references, the main points as full sentences, and the one line that hits you. If writing pulls you out of listening entirely, record the message and take notes from the replay. The goal is to be formed by the sermon, and both approaches work if you do the after-step.

What should sermon notes include?

At minimum: the date and passage, every verse referenced, the two to four main points, a one-sentence summary in your own words, and one specific thing to do that week. The summary and the one thing are what make the notes worth rereading.

How do I remember a sermon after Sunday?

Write the message in one sentence the same day, pick one action with a day attached, and set a midweek reminder that shows you both. Rereading is what turns a sermon into change, and reminders are what make rereading happen.

The bottom line

Sermon notes fail when they are written for Sunday. Catch the passages, the points and one sentence during the message, then spend ten minutes the same day writing the summary, answering the question you brought, and choosing one thing to do. Reread monthly. That is the entire method, and it is the difference between hearing sermons and being changed by them.
