What Does the Bible Say About Drug Addiction? Hope and Help

November 27, 2026 · 8 min read · By Chris Hardaway
Photo via Pexels

It is 1 a.m. and a mother is typing a question into her phone that she would never say out loud at church: "What does the Bible say about drug addiction?" Her son is twenty-four. He has been clean for three months, then he wasn't. She wants to know if God is angry with him, if she did something wrong, and whether there is any hope left. Maybe you are her. Maybe you are the son.

This post answers that question plainly. The Bible never uses the word "addiction," but it talks a great deal about what addiction is: being mastered by something, losing control, hiding, and the long road back. It also talks about a God who goes down into the pit after people.

If you need help right now: call the SAMHSA National Helpline at 1-800-662-4357. It is free, confidential and open 24/7, in English and Spanish. If someone may be overdosing, call 911 immediately. If you are thinking about ending your life, call or text 988. Scripture is for the soul, but it is not a substitute for medical care, treatment or counseling.

What the Bible actually says about drugs and alcohol

Drugs as we know them did not exist in the ancient world, so you will not find a verse that names heroin, fentanyl or meth. Alcohol is the substance the Bible discusses most, and its teaching is consistent: drunkenness is condemned, and being controlled by anything other than God is a kind of slavery.

Ephesians 5:18: "And be not drunk with wine, wherein is excess; but be filled with the Spirit." Paul sets two kinds of control side by side. Something will fill you and steer you. The question is what.

Proverbs 23:29–35 reads like it was written by someone who sat with an addict. "Who hath woe? who hath sorrow? who hath contentions? who hath babbling? who hath wounds without cause? who hath redness of eyes? They that tarry long at the wine." It ends with the most honest line in the passage, the voice of someone who has been hurt and still cannot stop: "When shall I awake? I will seek it yet again" (v. 35). The Bible understood the cycle long before anyone called it a disorder.

1 Corinthians 6:12: "All things are lawful unto me, but all things are not expedient: all things are lawful for me, but I will not be brought under the power of any." The Greek verb for "brought under the power" is exousiazō (G1850), to have authority over. Paul's concern is not only what a thing is, but whether it rules you.

Some readers point to Galatians 5:20, where "witchcraft" translates the Greek pharmakeia (G5331), the word behind our "pharmacy." It is true that the word is connected to drugs and potions. But most scholars read it in context as sorcery and occult practice, not recreational drug use, so it is not the strongest verse to build a case on. The passages above are clearer.

Your body, your mind, and who you belong to

What? know ye not that your body is the temple of the Holy Ghost which is in you, which ye have of God, and ye are not your own? For ye are bought with a price: therefore glorify God in your body, and in your spirit, which are God's.

1 Corinthians 6:19–20 (KJV)

This passage is often used as a club: "Your body is a temple, so stop." Read it again. The center of it is not a rule but a price. You were bought. You are worth something to God, enough that He paid for you. Addiction tells a person they are worthless and only the next hit makes life bearable. Scripture says the opposite: you are valuable, and that is exactly why the thing destroying you matters.

1 Peter 5:8: "Be sober, be vigilant; because your adversary the devil, as a roaring lion, walketh about, seeking whom he may devour." The word for "sober" is nēphō (G3525), to be clear-headed, and "vigilant" is grēgoreō (G1127), to stay awake. Peter is not only talking about substances, but the principle holds: a clouded mind is easier prey.

Is addiction a sin or a disease?

This is where Christians genuinely disagree, and it is worth laying out fairly.

  • Addiction as a spiritual problem. Some emphasize that choices are involved, that Scripture calls drunkenness sin (Galatians 5:21, 1 Corinthians 6:10), and that the deepest answer is repentance and a new heart.
  • Addiction as a medical condition. Others emphasize what medicine has learned about how substances change the brain, and see addiction as an illness that needs treatment, the way you would treat diabetes.
  • Both at once. Many pastors, counselors and recovery ministries hold both: the person is responsible for their choices and is also caught in something that has rewired their body and is bigger than willpower.

The Bible itself gives room for the "both" view. Paul describes a person who knows what is right and cannot do it: "For the good that I would I do not: but the evil which I would not, that I do" (Romans 7:19). He was not writing about drugs, but anyone in recovery recognizes that sentence. Sin is real, and so is bondage. The answer to both is not shame. It is rescue.

A practical note: using medicine to treat addiction is not a lack of faith. Paul told Timothy to take a little wine "for thy stomach's sake and thine often infirmities" (1 Timothy 5:23), using what was available as medicine. Doctors, treatment programs and prescribed medication can be part of how God answers prayer.

Freedom is the point

Galatians 5:1: "Stand fast therefore in the liberty wherewith Christ hath made us free, and be not entangled again with the yoke of bondage." The gospel is not a list of things you can no longer enjoy. It is a jailbreak. Jesus put it this way: "If the Son therefore shall make you free, ye shall be free indeed" (John 8:36).

Freedom rarely comes all at once. Many people in recovery relapse, sometimes more than once. That does not mean God has given up. 1 Corinthians 10:13 says "God is faithful, who will not suffer you to be tempted above that ye are able; but will with the temptation also make a way to escape." The way of escape is often very ordinary: a phone call, a meeting, a friend who answers, a different route home.

The God who goes into the pit

Psalm 40 may be the best recovery psalm in the Bible.

Psalm 40:1–3: "I waited patiently for the LORD; and he inclined unto me, and heard my cry. He brought me up also out of an horrible pit, out of the miry clay, and set my feet upon a rock, and established my goings. And he hath put a new song in my mouth, even praise unto our God: many shall see it, and fear, and shall trust in the LORD."

The "pit" is bowr (H953), a cistern or dungeon, and "horrible" is sha'own (H7588), a word for roaring or ruin. Notice the order. The psalmist does not climb out and then call God. He cries from the bottom, and God bends down. Then God does three things: lifts, steadies, and gives a new song. Recovery has the same shape. Getting out is the first step; learning to walk on the rock is the long work after.

And when someone comes home after falling again, the father in Jesus' parable does not wait at the door with a lecture. "When he was yet a great way off, his father saw him, and had compassion, and ran, and fell on his neck, and kissed him" (Luke 15:20).

No condemnation, and a lot of company

Romans 8:1: "There is therefore now no condemnation to them which are in Christ Jesus." Shame is fuel for addiction. It says: hide this, use again to stop feeling it. Grace breaks the cycle by bringing it into the light.

That is why the Bible puts so much weight on community. "Confess your faults one to another, and pray one for another, that ye may be healed" (James 5:16). "Bear ye one another's burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ" (Galatians 6:2). Nobody gets free alone. Christ-centered groups like Celebrate Recovery, along with Alcoholics Anonymous and Narcotics Anonymous, meet in thousands of churches, and many people find their footing there alongside a counselor or treatment program.

If you love someone who is addicted

  • It is not your fault, and you cannot fix it alone. You can love, pray, and set boundaries. You cannot make someone sober.
  • Keep them alive. Learn the signs of overdose. If opioids are involved, ask a pharmacist about naloxone, and call 911 in an emergency.
  • Get support for yourself. Talk to your pastor, a counselor, or a family support group. Call SAMHSA at 1-800-662-4357 for referrals.
  • Do not stop praying. The father in Luke 15 kept watching the road.

One step to take this week

If you are struggling, tell one person the truth this week: a pastor, a friend, a doctor, or the SAMHSA line. That is James 5:16 in practice. Then read Psalm 40:1–3 out loud each morning and pray it in your own words: "Lord, I'm in the pit. Hear me. Lift me. Set my feet somewhere solid." His mercies "are new every morning" (Lamentations 3:23), including mornings after a relapse.

FAQ

Is drug addiction a sin according to the Bible?

The Bible does not use the word addiction. It condemns drunkenness (Ephesians 5:18, Galatians 5:21) and warns against being brought under the power of anything (1 Corinthians 6:12). Christians differ on how to describe addiction: many see it as both a sin issue and a medical condition. Either way, Scripture's answer is grace, help and freedom, not shame.

Does God forgive relapse?

Yes. Romans 8:1 says there is no condemnation for those in Christ, and Lamentations 3:22-23 says God's mercies are new every morning. In Luke 15 the father runs to meet his returning son. Relapse is a reason to reach out again, not a sign that God has given up.

What Bible verse helps with addiction recovery?

Psalm 40:1-3 is a favorite: God hears the cry from the horrible pit, lifts the person out, and sets their feet on a rock. 1 Corinthians 10:13, Galatians 5:1 and John 8:36 are also widely used in recovery.

Is it wrong for a Christian to take medication for addiction?

No. Using medicine is not a lack of faith. Paul told Timothy to take a little wine for his stomach (1 Timothy 5:23), using what was available as medicine. Doctors, treatment and prescribed medication can be part of how God answers prayer. For referrals, call SAMHSA at 1-800-662-4357.

The bottom line

The Bible does not name modern drugs, but it knows addiction well: the loss of control, the hiding, the cry from the pit. Its answer is not shame. It is a God who hears, lifts and steadies, and a community that carries burdens together. Tell someone the truth this week, and call 1-800-662-4357 if you need help finding treatment.
