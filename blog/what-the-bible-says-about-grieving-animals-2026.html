What the Bible Says About Grieving Animals, After Fiona the Hippo

November 24, 2026 · 8 min read · By Chris Hardaway
Photo via Pexels

On Thursday, November 19, a lot of grown adults in Cincinnati cried over a hippo. Some of them were a little embarrassed about it. If you were one of them, or if you are still carrying the loss of a dog, a cat or a horse you loved, this post is for you. The question underneath the tears is an old one: does God care about animals, and is it all right to grieve one?

The short answer from Scripture is yes, and yes. The longer answer is worth reading slowly.

What happened to Fiona

Fiona was born six weeks premature at the Cincinnati Zoo in January 2017. Her care team kept her alive through those first fragile months while people around the world followed her progress online. She became something rare: an animal a whole city claimed as its own. There were children's books about her, an ice cream flavor, a mural downtown.

The week before her death, a hat ended up in her habitat by accident and she swallowed it. The zoo says its team examined her with a scope, tried a vibration plate to help move the hat through her digestive tract, and treated her around the clock. On November 19 the zoo announced that she had died. Director Dave Jenike said, "She fought until she couldn't anymore." Cincinnati Mayor Aftab Pureval called her "a symbol of perseverance and joy." You can read the reporting from the Cincinnati Zoo, NBC News and ABC News.

The zoo asked people to keep the staff who tried to save her in their thoughts and prayers. That is a good place for believers to start: the people who cared for her every day are grieving the most.

The question it raises

Moments like this bring up questions people rarely say out loud. Is it silly to feel this sad about an animal? Does God notice? Did her life matter to Him? And for anyone who has buried a pet, the question behind all of them: will I see my animal again?

Photo via Pexels

Christians have not all answered the last question the same way, and we will be honest about that below. But the first three have clear answers in the Bible.

What Scripture says about God and animals

1. God made them and called them good

Before there was a single human being, God made "every living creature that moveth" and "saw that it was good" (Genesis 1:21). The Hebrew phrase for "living creature," nephesh chayyah, uses the same word, nephesh (H5315), that describes Adam as "a living soul" in Genesis 2:7. That does not mean animals and humans are the same; only humans are made in God's image (Genesis 1:27). It does mean that animal life is not an afterthought. God gave it breath on purpose.

2. God made the hippo and was proud of it

Here is a detail most readers skip. Near the end of the book of Job, when God finally answers Job out of the whirlwind, He does not explain Job's suffering. He points to His creatures. And He spends a long passage on one animal in particular.

Behold now behemoth, which I made with thee; he eateth grass as an ox. Lo now, his strength is in his loins, and his force is in the navel of his belly. … He is the chief of the ways of God.

Job 40:15–16, 19 (KJV)

The Hebrew word is behemoth (H930), a plural form of the ordinary word for a beast, used here for one enormous animal. What it refers to is debated. Many scholars and translators identify it with the hippopotamus, which lived along the Nile and eats grass and lies "under the shady trees, in the covert of the reed, and fens" (Job 40:21). Others suggest the elephant, and some read it as a poetic or symbolic creature. The text does not settle it. Either way, the point of the passage is plain: God made this great beast, delights in it, and calls it "the chief of the ways of God." He is not embarrassed by His animals. He shows them off.

3. Not one of them is forgotten

Jesus used the cheapest animals in the market to make a point about God's attention. "Are not five sparrows sold for two farthings, and not one of them is forgotten before God?" (Luke 12:6). And again: "one of them shall not fall on the ground without your Father" (Matthew 10:29). His argument runs from smaller to greater: if God notices sparrows, He certainly notices you. But the smaller half of that argument is true too. God notices sparrows.

The psalms say the same thing in other words. "The LORD is good to all: and his tender mercies are over all his works" (Psalm 145:9). "O LORD, thou preservest man and beast" (Psalm 36:6). Psalm 104 is an entire hymn about God feeding lions, giving water to wild donkeys and filling "this great and wide sea" with "both small and great beasts" (Psalm 104:25). Even at the end of Jonah, God's last word of mercy toward Nineveh mentions "also much cattle" (Jonah 4:11).

4. Caring for animals is part of being righteous

"A righteous man regardeth the life of his beast" (Proverbs 12:10). God's covenant after the flood was made not only with Noah but "with every living creature that is with you" (Genesis 9:10). The people who looked after Fiona through her premature birth and her last week were doing something the Bible honors.

Is it okay to grieve an animal?

Yes. When the prophet Nathan wanted to reach King David's heart, he told a story about a poor man's one little ewe lamb, which "grew up together with him, and with his children; it did eat of his own meat, and drank of his own cup, and lay in his bosom, and was unto him as a daughter" (2 Samuel 12:3). Nathan knew David would feel that loss, and David did. Scripture treats the bond between a person and an animal as real enough to build a sermon on.

Grieving an animal is not a failure of perspective. It is a sign that you noticed something good God made, and you loved it. Paul tells us to "weep with them that weep" (Romans 12:15), and he does not add an exception for the kind of loss that seems small to someone else.

A pastoral note: grief over an animal can hit hard, especially for people who live alone, children, and anyone already carrying other losses. Scripture is for the soul, not a substitute for care. If sadness is staying heavy for weeks, talk to a pastor, a counselor or a doctor.

Do animals go to heaven?

Here is where honesty matters. The Bible does not directly say whether individual animals live again, and Christians have held different views for centuries.

  • Many believers expect animals in the new creation. Isaiah pictures a world where "the wolf also shall dwell with the lamb" (Isaiah 11:6). Paul writes that "the creature itself also shall be delivered from the bondage of corruption" and that "the whole creation groaneth and travaileth in pain together until now" (Romans 8:21–22). The Greek word for creature or creation, ktisis (G2937), is broad. Some read this as including the animals we have known.
  • Others are more cautious. They note that the Bible ties resurrection to human beings made in God's image, and that passages like Isaiah 11 may describe animal life in the new creation without promising that particular animals return. Ecclesiastes 3:21 asks openly, "Who knoweth the spirit of man that goeth upward, and the spirit of the beast that goeth downward to the earth?" and leaves the question unanswered.

What every tradition agrees on is this: the God who will make all things new is good, and He will "wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying" (Revelation 21:4). Whatever the details, no one in that world will feel cheated. You can rest your grief on God's character even where the Bible leaves a question open.

One thing to do this week

Read Psalm 104 slowly, out loud if you can. It takes about five minutes. Each time it names an animal, thank God for one creature you have loved or one you have seen and marveled at. Then pray for the people who lost Fiona: her keepers, her veterinarians and the families who watched her grow up. If you are grieving an animal of your own, tell God about it plainly. He made it, and He is not too busy to listen.

FAQ

Does the Bible say God cares about animals?

Yes. Psalm 145:9 says God's tender mercies are over all his works, Psalm 36:6 says he preserves man and beast, and Jesus said not one sparrow is forgotten before God (Luke 12:6). Proverbs 12:10 adds that a righteous person regards the life of his animal.

Is the behemoth in Job a hippo?

Many scholars and translators think so, because Job 40 describes a huge grass-eating animal that lies among the reeds and fens. Others suggest the elephant or read it as a poetic creature. The Bible does not settle it, but the passage clearly shows God delighting in a great animal he made.

Is it wrong to grieve a pet as a Christian?

No. Scripture treats the bond between people and animals as real; Nathan's parable of the little ewe lamb in 2 Samuel 12:3 depends on it. Grief is a sign you loved something good God made. If the sadness stays heavy for weeks, talk with a pastor or counselor.

Will I see my pet in heaven?

The Bible does not say directly, and Christians differ. Some point to Isaiah 11:6 and Romans 8:21-22 and expect animals in the new creation; others are more cautious. All agree that God will wipe away every tear (Revelation 21:4), so you can trust his goodness where the details are open.

The bottom line

Fiona's life and death remind us of something the Bible says plainly: God made the animals, called them good, notices every sparrow and once pointed to a great river beast as the chief of his ways. Grieving an animal is not foolish. Bring the sadness to God, pray for those who cared for her, and trust his goodness where Scripture leaves a question open.
